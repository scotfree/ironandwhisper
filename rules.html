Iron and Whispers

A game of occupation and sedition, for two players

A deck of Rebel Agent cards and a fistful of Imperial Troop tokens struggle for control over road-connected Locations.

OneThe Struggle

One player is an Empire and plays entirely in the open: marching, recruiting, and garrisoning Troops. The other is a Rebel Conspiracy working in secret to place Agents and Misinformation.

Both seek to build their Presence in a location. When the Place finally has to choose a side, it's the one with the most Presence. The other side loses whatever resources it had there. Losing Presence (as Agents or Troops) gives the other side Points. The game ends when all locations have chosen a side and the one with the most points wins.

TwoPlaying as The Empire

As the Empire your turns look like:

  1. Optionally force one location to Choose a Side
  2. Build New Troops in any City
  3. Move Troops
  4. Stationary Troops reveal one Agent
  5. Remove any Starving Troops.

Note that you must supply and support your Troops or they will starve! Each Place you occupy (have at least one Troop in) will generate TWO supply points. The points for a connected network of Places are all pooled and available to any Troops in the network. If there's not enough, Troops must be removed starting with the highest troop concentrations.

ThreePlaying as The Rebels

As the Rebels your turns look like:

  1. Force one Place to Choose a Side
  2. Place Agent cards face down on any Place.

You can examine your own decks any time, but they are hidden from the Empire player. When the Empire reveals an Agent in a place, you must turn the top card in that deck and leave it in a separate face-up deck in the same place. You can use your zero-presence agent cards as "Decoys" since the Empire gets no points for defeating them.

You only get points when the other side loses Presence. The Empire must capture locations with real Agents in them. The Rebels must destroy Troops by conquest or starvation.

SixThe rules

What you need

  • The board: twelve locations joined by seventeen roads, in a three-by-four lattice. Two of the locations — Everlan and Kirn — are cities, at opposite ends of the middle column.
  • A deck of 60 agent cards: 36 worth 1 presence and 24 worth nothing. The backs must be identical.
  • Around a dozen troop tokens in one colour.
  • Markers for a resolved location in each side's colour, and something to keep score with.

Setting up

  1. Put 3 troops in Everlan and 1 in Belmar. No agents are on the board.
  2. Shuffle the deck. The Insurgency draws a hand of 3.
  3. The Insurgency takes the first turn. Players then alternate.

Because the whole hand is placed every turn and the deck never reshuffles, the game is exactly 20 Insurgency turns long unless it ends sooner.

Taking a turn

A turn is not one action. You do everything your side can do, in order. Both sides begin the same way, and it is the most important ordering in the game.

Resolution comes first, and it is judged on the board as your opponent left it. You cannot march into a location and cash it in on arrival, and you cannot place exactly enough agents and collect in the same breath. Whatever you commit has to survive a reply before it pays.

The Rebels

  1. You may declare a resolution on one location where you have at least one agent in the pile. It settles at once, on the pile as it already stands — this turn's cards are not down yet and do not count.
  2. Place your entire hand face down, into any unresolved locations you like. Any number into the same location, in whatever order you choose; the last one placed goes on top. You may not hold a card back. A location you resolved in step 1 is closed and takes none of them.
  3. Draw back up to three.

The Empire

  1. You may declare a resolution on one location where you have at least one troop, against the agents already standing there.
  2. Build: raise up to one troop in each city you hold. You may build past your supply ceiling.
  3. March: any or all troops may move one road. Nobody has to move. A garrison that just won its location in step 1 may march straight out of it.
  4. Look: every troop that did not move turns one agent in its location face up.
  5. Starve: see below. This is the only step you do not choose.

Resolving a location

Either player may resolve one location per turn, free of charge, and only a location they are actually in — the Empire needs a troop there, the Insurgency needs an agent in the pile. You cannot force a confrontation somewhere you do not exist.

  1. Turn the location's whole pile face up. It stays that way.
  2. Add up the Insurgency's presence: the values of all its agents there, face up and face down alike. Blanks count nothing.
  3. Add up the Empire's presence: 1 for each troop standing there.
  4. The higher total takes the location. The Empire wins ties.
  5. Mark the location with the winner's colour. It is finished for the rest of the game.

Scoring

The winner scores the presence the loser had committed, and nothing else. There are no points for the location itself.

So the Insurgency scores the troops it destroys, and the Empire scores the real agents it captures. Three consequences are worth saying out loud, because they shape every decision in the game:

  • Walkovers pay nothing. Taking an empty location scores zero. It may still be worth doing — it denies the ground — but it does not move the score.
  • The bigger the enemy's commitment, the bigger your score. The ideal resolution wins by one against everything the opponent has.
  • A pile of blanks has teeth. Bait the Empire into marching an army at a location worth nothing, and even winning the resolution pays it nothing at all — while the troops it moved were needed somewhere else.

Afterwards, the loser's commitment leaves the board and the winner's stays. Captured agents are removed; a losing garrison is removed. A winning garrison remains where it is. The winner's agents stay face up on the location as a permanent record and do nothing further.

Supply, building and starvation

Each location the Empire occupies contributes 2 supply, and occupied locations that touch pool theirs into one network. Each troop costs 1 supply to keep standing, so a network of four locations supports eight troops wherever in the chain they are.

Supply limits what the Empire can keep, not what it can build. Raising troops past the ceiling is legal and often right — you may build in a city and march the new troops out to the ground that will feed them in the same turn.

At the end of the Empire's turn, any network holding more troops than it can feed is marked with the number that will die. If that network is still short at the end of the Empire's next turn, those troops are removed and the Insurgency scores them. The Empire may say which locations pay; otherwise the loss falls on the largest garrisons first.

A location the rebels have won contributes nothing to any network, for ever. That is the Insurgency's second way of killing troops: not by beating them, but by cutting the ground out from under them.

How the game ends

Four things end it, and all four do the same thing: every location still open resolves at once, by the normal rules, and all the points are scored.

  • The deck runs out and the Insurgency cannot refill its hand. This is the clock, and it is exact.
  • Every location has been resolved. There is nothing left to play for.
  • The Empire is eliminated — no troops anywhere and no city left that will build it any. It can never act again.
  • Both players offer to end. Either may put up a standing offer and take it down again; when both are up, the game stops. This is not a pass — you cannot skip a turn, because that would stop the clock.

In the final sweep, a location neither side ever set foot in — no troops, no agents — is simply left open. You may only resolve a location you are in, and that holds for the sweep as much as for anything else.

Whoever has the most points wins.
